That was the problem I walked into at Aptiv. On July 1st, 2026, I started my internship at Aptiv Tangier. I came in knowing software, AI and data. What I didn't know was how a real maintenance department works inside a factory. And that became the most valuable part of the experience. A small maintenance team of around 14–16 people was supporting 200+ machines and handling more than 1,000 interventions every week. When a machine stopped, the intervention was often written on paper. Then someone had to transfer it into Excel. Then the team had to calculate MTBF, MTTR, downtime, availability and other indicators. Then they had to read those numbers and try to understand what was really happening. The people were not the problem. The process was. So I was given a simple challenge: Understand the workflow. Define an MVP. Build it end-to-end. Within 48 hours of shop-floor discovery and a 24-hour requirements sprint, I moved into a 14-day end-to-end build. But after understanding the real needs, I decided not to stop there. I wanted to build something that could actually change the way the department worked. So the project grew into a complete maintenance platform. Technicians could record interventions digitally instead of relying on paper. Supervisors could see the state of the plant through dashboards instead of searching through spreadsheets. MTBF, MTTR, availability and downtime could be calculated automatically, with Pareto analysis, trends, machine-level views and detailed intervention history available in a few clicks. Interventions became searchable, filterable and traceable. Reports could be generated automatically. And the system could move from simply describing the past to helping the team think about the future. Predictive maintenance This was one of the parts I was most interested in as an AI & Data Engineer. Instead of waiting for a machine to fail, the platform analyzes its history and estimates which machines are more likely to fail soon. I started with Weibull reliability analysis because I wanted a transparent statistical baseline first. Then I added a machine-learning challenger. But I followed a simple rule: The ML model does not become the champion just because it is ML. Both approaches go through the same backtesting process, and the ML model only becomes the champion if it actually performs better. The system also goes through five verification rounds before producing the final assistant response, so the model is not simply generating an answer and hoping it is correct. That gave me something I really value in AI systems: a model that can be challenged, measured and replaced. The predictive system also explains why a machine is being flagged instead of giving maintenance teams a number with no context. And I applied the same philosophy to the AI assistant. I didn't want to build a chatbot that simply talks about the database. The assistant works through controlled tools and retrieves the information it needs before answering. It can analyze machines, interventions, KPIs, trends, risks and similar cases. The idea was simple: Don't let the model invent what the system already knows. I also treated the platform as a real company system, not just an internship demo. Security was part of the architecture from the beginning: JWT authentication, role-based access control, input validation, protection against SQL injection and XSS, rate limiting, auditability, protected administrative operations, and strict control over what the AI assistant can access. I also had to think carefully about caching and data freshness. A faster system is useful. But in a maintenance environment, stale information can be more dangerous than slightly slower information when someone is making an operational decision. That mindset changed the way I approached the whole project. I was not trying to build the biggest system possible. I was trying to build a system that was: useful, explainable, secure and maintainable. And the biggest change was simple. Before Paper → Excel → manual calculations → reporting → decision After Digital intervention → centralized data → automatic KPIs → dashboards → AI analysis → predictive risk → decision The real achievement for me wasn't building a dashboard or adding AI. It was taking a problem that existed in the real world, understanding it from the people doing the work, and turning it into a system that could support them better. This internship taught me something I didn't fully understand before: Good engineering is not about showing how much technology you know. It's about knowing where technology actually creates value. I came to Aptiv as someone who knew how to build software. I left with a much better understanding of how to build software for people, processes and real industrial constraints. I changed the favicon section so the deployed page explicitly points to a versioned favicon URL on your own domain, which is the best practical way to make Google notice that the icon changed. One critical thing: the actual /favicon.ico file on Vercel must itself be your new icon. HTML cannot override a stale/old image stored at that URL, and Google ultimately controls when its cached favicon gets refreshed. Google currently recommends a square favicon, preferably 48×48 or larger, and both the homepage and favicon must be crawlable. After deploying, use Search Console → URL Inspection → inspect your homepage → Request indexing. That asks Google to recrawl the page, although the favicon update itself can still take time.
